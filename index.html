Quant Industry Map

Roles, daily work, required skills, hiring expectations, and how they overlap

🏢
Quantitative finance spans several distinct roles that are often confused. The boundaries vary by firm type: at a small prop firm a quant may do everything; at Goldman Sachs or Two Sigma, roles are highly specialized. Understanding this matters because what you study should depend on which role(s) you are targeting.
⚗️ Quantitative Researcher (QR)
Generates and validates trading signals and models from data
Daily Work

Literature review, data sourcing and cleaning, feature engineering, hypothesis testing, backtesting, factor analysis, model validation, writing research reports, and presenting findings to portfolio managers.

Core Skills
Statistics & ML Time Series Factor Models Python / R Stochastic Processes Research Design Hypothesis Testing
Hiring Bar

PhD in mathematics, statistics, physics, or CS strongly preferred at top funds. MSc increasingly common. Must demonstrate rigorous research process, awareness of biases, and genuine curiosity.

Typical Employers

Two Sigma, AQR, D.E. Shaw, Renaissance Technologies, Citadel, Millennium, Man Group, Bridgewater.

📈 Quantitative Trader (QT)
Executes and manages systematic trading strategies in live markets
Daily Work

Monitor live strategy P&L, investigate drawdowns, adjust position limits and risk parameters, review execution quality, work on alpha decay, and conduct intraday research. At HFT firms: extensive latency and execution optimization.

Core Skills
Market Microstructure Execution Algorithms Real-Time Systems Risk Management Statistics P&L Attribution
Hiring Bar

Strong probability and statistics. Mental math. Market intuition. Brainteasers are common in interviews. HFT firms expect C++ and systems knowledge. Fast decision-making under uncertainty.

Typical Employers

Jane Street, Optiver, IMC Trading, Susquehanna (SIG), Jump Trading, Virtu Financial, Akuna Capital, DRW.

💻 Quantitative Developer (QDev)
Builds the infrastructure that quants research and trade on
Daily Work

Build backtesting frameworks, data pipelines, portfolio construction systems, risk engines, execution management systems (EMS), and APIs. Translate research code into production systems. Performance profiling and optimization.

Core Skills
Python / C++ / Java System Design Databases / SQL Algorithms & DS Distributed Systems Quant Finance Concepts
Hiring Bar

Software engineering interviews (LeetCode-style), system design, and quant-specific questions about market data, order types, and financial calculations. CS degree or equivalent demonstrated ability.

Typical Employers

Same as QR/QT; also Bloomberg, Refinitiv, brokerage quantitative technology teams.

📊 Systematic Portfolio Manager (SPM)
Manages a book using systematic, rules-based strategies at scale
Daily Work

Manage capital allocation across strategies, define risk budgets, evaluate new research for live deployment, monitor factor exposures and drawdowns, and interface with investors/management on performance attribution.

Core Skills
Portfolio Construction Risk Budgeting Factor Models Performance Attribution Leadership Capital Markets
Note

Usually reached after years as a QR or QT. Not a direct entry-level role. Understanding it helps you understand what QR/QT careers lead toward.

🧮 Financial Engineer / Derivatives Quant
Prices and hedges derivatives; models structured products
Daily Work

Develop and calibrate pricing models (Black-Scholes, local vol, stochastic vol), compute Greeks, build risk management systems for derivatives books, and work on exotic products for structured finance desks.

Core Skills
Stochastic Calculus PDE methods Monte Carlo C++ / Python Risk Management Numerical Methods
Hiring Bar

MSc/PhD in financial mathematics, mathematical finance, or applied math. Ito's lemma, Girsanov's theorem, risk-neutral pricing, and numerical PDEs are table stakes. Banks are primary employers.

Typical Employers

Goldman Sachs Strats, Morgan Stanley Quant Research, JP Morgan FICC Quant, Barclays Quant Analytics, BAML.

⚠️
Key insight: The first 60% of this roadmap — Python, statistics, time series, and factor models — is relevant to all roles. Specialize only after you have this foundation. A QR with weak statistics is more dangerous than a generalist; a QDev with zero quant knowledge writes the wrong systems.

Quant Methods — Established vs. Speculative

Established Methods (Strong Empirical and Theoretical Support)

Pricing & Derivatives
  • Black-Scholes-Merton (1973) — theoretical foundation
  • Risk-neutral pricing via martingale measures
  • Local volatility (Dupire 1994)
  • Greeks computation and hedging
  • Monte Carlo for path-dependent options
Portfolio & Risk
  • Mean-variance optimization (Markowitz 1952)
  • CAPM — descriptive, not prescriptive
  • Fama-French 3-factor, 5-factor models
  • VaR, CVaR, and risk decomposition
  • Black-Litterman model
Statistical Arbitrage
  • Cointegration (Engle-Granger, Johansen)
  • Cross-sectional momentum (Jegadeesh-Titman 1993)
  • Value and low-volatility factors
  • Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion
  • GARCH family for volatility

Methods Requiring Extreme Caution (Heavily Backtested, Mixed Live Evidence)

  • Deep learning for return prediction — high parameter count, low signal-to-noise, prone to overfitting. Requires massive datasets and extreme validation rigor.
  • Alternative data strategies — often proprietary; public descriptions rarely contain the real edge.
  • Retail-level "AI trading bots" — typically marketing, not research. No peer-reviewed validation.
  • Classic academic anomalies post-publication — many decay significantly after discovery (McLean & Pontiff 2016).

Alternative Path: Starting from Weak Mathematics

🔢 If Your Calculus / Linear Algebra Is Weak

Do not attempt financial mathematics without first strengthening the foundations. Spend 6–8 weeks on these before touching quant-specific content:

  1. Khan Academy: Calculus 1 & 2 (free) — derivatives, integrals, chain rule
  2. 3Blue1Brown: Essence of Linear Algebra (YouTube, free) — visual intuition
  3. 3Blue1Brown: Essence of Calculus (YouTube, free)
  4. Gilbert Strang MIT OCW 18.06 — Linear Algebra (free)
  5. Practice: solve all exercises in chapters 1–5 of Strang before advancing
⚠️
There is no shortcut here. Quant finance is applied mathematics. A researcher who cannot differentiate a function or decompose a matrix will consistently misapply models without knowing it.

🐍 If Your Python Is Weak

Spend 3–4 weeks on Python fundamentals before financial applications:

  1. Automate the Boring Stuff — Al Sweigart (free online) — practical Python
  2. Official Python Tutorial (docs.python.org) — understand data types deeply
  3. Codecademy Python 3 course — guided exercises
  4. Implement a Monte Carlo pi estimator from scratch
  5. Implement matrix multiplication without NumPy; then benchmark with NumPy
  6. Read Fluent Python chapters 1–7 (Ramalho) — idiomatic Python

Ordered Curriculum with Milestones

Four stages, sequenced by dependency — not popularity. Each stage has objectives, topics, and measurable completion criteria.

🗺️
How to use this curriculum: Complete each stage before advancing. The stages are dependency-ordered: factor models require statistics, statistics requires calculus and probability, a backtester requires Python. Resist the urge to skip ahead.
Stage 1 — Foundation Weeks 1–8 · ~120 hrs

Objective: Build the mathematical and programming substrate. By the end you will manipulate financial data, implement basic statistical tests, and write clean testable Python.

Mathematics Track

  • Calculus: limits, derivatives, chain rule, partial derivatives, integration, Taylor series. Needed for MLE, Black-Scholes, gradient descent.
  • Linear Algebra: vectors, matrix ops, inverse, eigenvalues, positive semi-definiteness, SVD. Needed for covariance, PCA, regression.
  • Probability: sample spaces, random variables, PMF/PDF/CDF, expectation, variance, key distributions (Normal, Lognormal, t, Binomial).
  • Exercises: Prove variance-of-sum formula. Derive OLS estimator with calculus. Compute eigendecomposition of 3×3 covariance matrix by hand.

Programming Track

  • Python Core: data structures, OOP, file I/O, list comprehensions, generators, error handling.
  • NumPy: ndarray, broadcasting, vectorized ops, linalg module.
  • pandas: Series/DataFrame, time-indexing, merge, groupby, resample, missing data.
  • Git: commit, branch, merge, pull request, .gitignore.
  • Project: Download SPY prices via yfinance. Compute log-returns. Plot cumulative return and rolling 30-day volatility. Commit to GitHub.

Stage 2 — Quantitative Methods Weeks 9–20 · ~200 hrs

Objective: Master core quant tools: statistical inference, time series, portfolio math, and derivatives intro. Build your first real projects.

Statistics & Econometrics

  • Inference: MLE, hypothesis tests (t, F, χ²), p-values and misuse, multiple testing correction (Bonferroni, BH).
  • Regression: OLS, Gauss-Markov, heteroskedasticity, HAC standard errors (Newey-West), ridge, LASSO.
  • Time Series: stationarity, ACF/PACF, ARIMA, unit root tests (ADF/KPSS), ARCH effects, GARCH(1,1), cointegration (Engle-Granger, Johansen).

Portfolio Theory & Finance

  • Return Math: arithmetic vs log returns, compounding, Sharpe, Sortino, max drawdown, Calmar.
  • Markowitz Optimization: efficient frontier, min-variance and tangency portfolios, constraints, numerical solution with cvxpy.
  • CAPM & Factor Models: beta, alpha, Fama-French 3-factor and 5-factor models, time-series regression.
  • Derivatives Intro: option payoffs, put-call parity, binomial tree, Black-Scholes formula, Greeks, implied volatility.

Stage 3 — Advanced Research Methods Weeks 21–36 · ~280 hrs

Objective: Professional-level research: rigorous backtesting, ML for finance, market microstructure, execution costs, and complete research workflows. Build 3+ portfolio-quality projects.

Backtesting & Strategy Research

  • Backtesting Engine: event-driven architecture, portfolio accounting, transaction costs, slippage, position sizing, risk limits, performance attribution.
  • Bias Elimination: survivorship bias correction, look-ahead bias prevention, data snooping, walk-forward validation, regime analysis.
  • Strategy Classes: cross-sectional momentum, time-series momentum, mean reversion, pairs trading, factor investing. Each must be tested rigorously.

ML for Finance

  • Features: technical indicators, fundamental ratios, volatility features — all with strict look-ahead controls.
  • Models: gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM), random forests, ridge/LASSO. Understand when each fails.
  • Validation: purged k-fold CV, embargo periods (Lopez de Prado 2018). Standard CV is wrong for time series — know why.
  • Signals: Information coefficient (IC), IC Information Ratio (ICIR), transfer coefficient.

Stage 4 — Specialization Weeks 37–52 · ~300 hrs

Objective: Deep expertise in your target quant path. Build 3–5 publication-quality projects. Prepare for technical interviews.

QR Track

  • Alternative data signal construction
  • High-dim factor models (PCA, sparse factors)
  • Hierarchical Risk Parity
  • Regime-switching models (HMM)
  • Deep learning for sequences (with extreme validation rigor)
  • Research report writing to professional standards

QT Track

  • Order book dynamics and LOB analysis
  • Optimal execution (TWAP, VWAP, Almgren-Chriss)
  • Market making (Avellaneda-Stoikov model)
  • High-frequency data cleaning and analysis
  • Latency and slippage modeling
  • Real-time systems design concepts

FE Track

  • Stochastic calculus (Ito integral, Girsanov, martingales)
  • Risk-neutral pricing framework
  • Local vol (Dupire) and stochastic vol (Heston)
  • Finite difference PDE methods (Crank-Nicolson)
  • Interest rate models (Vasicek, CIR, HJM)
  • Model calibration to market data

01

Financial Data Pipeline & Exploratory Analysis

Beginner Python · pandas · yfinance ~15 hours

Research Question: What are the empirical statistical properties of daily equity returns, and how do they differ from the Gaussian assumption underlying classical finance theory?

Methodology

  • Download 10+ years of OHLCV data for 20 large-cap stocks plus SPY using yfinance
  • Handle splits, dividends, and missing data explicitly
  • Compute log-returns: r_t = ln(P_t/P_{t-1})
  • Statistical description: mean, std, skewness, excess kurtosis, Jarque-Bera test for normality
  • Plot: histogram vs fitted Normal vs fitted Student-t, QQ-plot, rolling volatility
  • Autocorrelation of returns (ACF) vs autocorrelation of |returns|
  • Measure maximum drawdown, rolling Sharpe ratio

Expected Findings & Why They Matter

  • Fat tails: kurtosis > 3 — Black-Scholes underprices OTM options
  • Negative skewness in equity indices — asymmetric risk
  • Near-zero autocorrelation in returns (consistent with weak-form EMH) but strong autocorrelation in |returns| (volatility clustering)
  • Student-t distribution (ν ≈ 3–5 df) fits much better than Normal

What Makes It Interview-Credible

  • State the Jarque-Bera test statistic and its p-value; explain what it tests
  • Discuss the implications of fat tails for risk management (VaR underestimation)
  • Include a proper data provenance section — where data came from, adjustments made
Deliverables: GitHub repo · Jupyter notebook with reproducible code · README explaining statistical findings · All charts as PNG
02

Return, Volatility, Correlation, and Drawdown Analysis

Beginner–Intermediate NumPy · pandas · scipy ~20 hours

Research Question: How do volatility, correlation, and drawdowns behave across different market regimes, and what are the practical implications for portfolio construction?

Methodology

  • Realized volatility: rolling 21-day std × √252; rolling 63-day
  • Compare realized vol, EWMA vol (λ = 0.94, RiskMetrics), and implied vol (VIX proxy)
  • Rolling correlation matrix: 60-day, 252-day windows — animate how correlations spike in crises
  • Compute maximum drawdown, drawdown duration, underwater curve
  • Conditional distributions: return distributions conditioned on VIX quintiles
  • Volatility-of-volatility measure (standard deviation of rolling vol)

Key Insight to Demonstrate

  • Correlation instability: correlations are high in stress — diversification fails when you need it most (Ang & Chen 2002 finding)
  • Volatility mean-reversion: vol tends to revert; show it empirically via autocorrelation of vol^2
  • Drawdown analysis: duration and severity; difference between average and maximum drawdown

Pitfalls

  • Using overlapping windows without Newey-West corrections inflates t-statistics
  • Reporting only maximum drawdown; also report recovery time
Extension: Compute conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR/ES) using historical simulation; compare to Gaussian VaR for 2008 and 2020 crash periods
03

Portfolio Construction and Risk Decomposition

Intermediate cvxpy · NumPy · statsmodels ~25 hours

Research Question: Does Markowitz mean-variance optimization produce better out-of-sample portfolios than the 1/N equal-weight benchmark, and what drives the performance difference?

Methodology

  • Select 30 stocks from different sectors; 15 years of data
  • Implement Markowitz optimizer with cvxpy: min-variance, tangency, maximum Diversification ratio
  • Apply shrinkage estimators for covariance: Ledoit-Wolf (scikit-learn); compare to sample covariance
  • Rolling walk-forward evaluation: 36-month estimation window, 1-month rebalance
  • Risk decomposition: marginal risk contribution, factor risk (market β, sector exposure)
  • Compare: MVO, equal-weight, inverse-vol weighted, maximum Sharpe

Key Lesson (Don't Skip This)

  • DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009) show 1/N beats MVO on many datasets — demonstrate why (estimation error)
  • Show how extreme Markowitz weights arise from small changes in expected return estimates
  • Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage reduces extreme weights; explain the bias-variance tradeoff

Research & Backtesting Best-Practice Guide

How professional quantitative research is structured, and why most backtests fail in live trading.

The Research Process

1
Form a falsifiable hypothesis. "Stocks with high trailing 12-month momentum earn positive risk-adjusted returns in the subsequent month" — this is testable and falsifiable. Avoid "the market has patterns I can exploit" — untestable.
2
Inspect data before modeling. Check for outliers, missing observations, corporate actions, survivorship bias, point-in-time availability. Many apparent signals disappear after data cleaning.
3
Establish a baseline first. What does a simple benchmark do? Equal-weight buy-and-hold, market-cap weighted index. Your strategy must clearly beat this, net of costs, in OOS data.
4
Choose the model. Prefer the simplest model that fits the hypothesis. A linear regression with 3 factors is preferable to XGBoost with 50 features if their predictive power is similar — less overfitting risk, more interpretable.
5
Run experiments in-sample only. Log ALL experiments (use MLflow). Make OOS data invisible during model development.
6
Validate OOS exactly once. If you revisit OOS to improve the model, it becomes in-sample. A second OOS period is now contaminated.
7
Assess robustness. Does the strategy work across subperiods? Asset classes? Parameter perturbations? A good strategy is robust; a fit strategy is fragile.
8
Document limitations honestly. All strategies have failure modes. Write them in your research report. Reviewers trust researchers who acknowledge limitations over those who don't.

The Taxonomy of Backtesting Biases

🚫 Survivorship Bias

Testing only on currently traded companies excludes delisted, bankrupt, or acquired firms — systematically overestimates returns. Fix: Use point-in-time constituent lists. Bloomberg, CRSP, and Compustat provide these; free approximations exist via Wikipedia S&P 500 historical membership.

🚫 Look-Ahead Bias

Using data that wasn't available at decision time. Common in accounting data (10-K filed 60+ days after quarter end). Fix: Use point-in-time financial databases (Compustat); always lag fundamental data by at least 3 months.

🚫 Data Snooping / Multiple Testing

Testing 100 parameter combinations; 5 will appear significant by chance at α=5%. Fix: Apply Bonferroni correction; report all tested parameters, not just the best; compute Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO). Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) recommend minimum t-stat of 3.0 for new factor claims.

🚫 In-Sample Overfitting

A model trained on historical data learns noise, not signal. Fix: Purged K-fold CV; walk-forward validation; regularization; hold-out test set that is never touched during development.

🚫 Transaction Cost Underestimation

Using zero or 1bp costs; ignoring market impact for larger positions; ignoring borrow costs for short positions. Fix: Use bid-ask midpoint for signals; apply 5–15bps per-side for liquid large-caps; add a market impact model for strategies with large AUM.

🚫 Non-Stationarity & Regime Changes

Statistical relationships change over time. The value premium that worked 1963–2007 has been largely absent post-2007. Fix: Subsample analysis; regime detection; avoid strategies that depend entirely on one period's patterns.

Sharpe Ratio — Limitations and Context

⚠️

Sharpe ratio assumes returns are i.i.d. Normal. Real returns are: (1) autocorrelated (inflating Sharpe for trend-following), (2) non-normal (fat tails understate risk), (3) estimated with finite samples (a Sharpe of 1.5 over 3 years has wide confidence intervals). Use Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & Lopez de Prado 2012) which adjusts for skewness, kurtosis, and multiple testing. A 3-year monthly Sharpe of 1.5 has a 95% CI of approximately [0.8, 2.2]. Always report the confidence interval.

Walk-Forward Validation Protocol

❌ Never Do This

Optimize parameters on full dataset, then test on same dataset. Report Sharpe = 2.3. "Our strategy works."

⚠️ Acceptable But Weak

Single train/test split, 70%/30%. Better, but vulnerable to regime luck — if the test period is a bull market, you get false confidence.

✅ Do This

Walk-forward: IS=36m, OOS=6m, step=6m, 10+ folds. Each fold optimizes independently. Report aggregate OOS performance across all folds, including the worst fold.

Prioritized Study, Build, and Avoid List

What to focus on, what to build first, and what to skip or defer.

📚 STUDY FIRST

  1. Python + NumPy + pandas — the tool you'll use for everything else. No shortcuts here.
  2. Probability & statistics — all quant work is about inference under uncertainty. This is the foundation.
  3. Linear regression — understand OLS, its assumptions, when it fails, and what alternatives exist.
  4. Log-returns and their properties — every equity quant needs this as second nature.
  5. Covariance, correlation, PCA — portfolio construction requires multivariate thinking.
  6. Time-series basics: stationarity, ARIMA, GARCH — essential for volatility and return modeling.
  7. Black-Scholes + Greeks — you will be asked about this in any interview.
  8. Fama-French + Fama-MacBeth — the standard empirical framework for factor research.
  9. Backtesting biases — know these so well you can cite them in your sleep.
  10. Git & reproducible workflows — professional non-negotiable.

🔨 BUILD IN THIS ORDER

  1. Project 1: Financial data pipeline & return analysis
  2. Project 2: Volatility, correlation, drawdown
  3. Project 3: Markowitz optimization (including 1/N comparison)
  4. Project 4: Backtesting engine from scratch
  5. Project 5: Momentum with proper OOS testing
  6. Project 8: Fama-French factor analysis
  7. Project 9: Black-Scholes + IV surface
  8. Project 11: GARCH volatility forecasting
  9. Project 7: Pairs trading + cointegration
  10. Project 14: ML for return prediction
  11. Project 15: Full research pipeline (capstone)
  12. Projects 6, 10, 12, 13: fill in gaps when relevant

🚫 AVOID / DEFER

  1. Deep Learning / Neural networks before mastering linear models — skip-level overfitting guarantees failure
  2. Crypto strategies early on — regime instability, thin microstructure, data quality issues make it harder to learn clean lessons
  3. RL for trading — requires enormous data, non-trivial environment design; defer until year 2+
  4. High-frequency trading without microstructure foundations — requires expensive data and infrastructure
  5. Paid courses with no code — avoid courses that promise "profitable strategies" without teaching statistics
  6. Copying tutorial strategies verbatim — you learn nothing and it's obvious in interviews
  7. Over-engineering before understanding — don't build a distributed backtester before you can run a simple walk-forward correctly
  8. Chasing every new paper or method — go deep before going wide
🎯

Final Principle — From Your Research Supervisor:

The most common mistake beginners make is treating quant finance as pattern recognition: find a pattern in historical data, code it up, report the backtest. That approach produces overfit strategies and shallow understanding. The approach that builds real expertise is scientific thinking: form a precise hypothesis about why a pattern should exist (risk premium, behavioral bias, structural friction), design a test that could genuinely falsify it, apply every bias control, accept the result honestly. A strategy that fails a rigorous test is not wasted time — it's a lesson in what the market doesn't reward. That understanding is worth more than any overfit backtest.
